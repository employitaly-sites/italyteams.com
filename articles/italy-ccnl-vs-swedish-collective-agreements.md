# Italian CCNL vs Swedish kollektivavtal: same idea, different default

> Swedish managers know collective agreements. The Italian CCNL works on a similar logic, with one big difference: it applies from the first hire.

If you run a company in Sweden, you already work inside a collective bargaining model. Pay floors, working time, occupational pension and insurance usually come from an agreement between a union and an employer organisation, not from law. Sweden has no statutory minimum wage, and agreements cover close to 90% of employees, according to the [National Mediation Office](https://www.mi.se/app/uploads/the-swedish-model-and-collective-agreements.pdf).

So when an Italian adviser tells you that "every job sits under a CCNL", it sounds familiar. It mostly is. But the small differences are the ones that cause wrong offers and payroll corrections later.

## What a CCNL is

CCNL stands for Contratto Collettivo Nazionale di Lavoro, the national collective labour agreement. Employer associations and trade unions negotiate one for each sector: commerce and services, metalworking, chemicals and pharma, textiles, logistics, hospitality and many more.

Like a Swedish agreement, it is not law. It is a negotiated text. In practice it is the reference for pay and conditions in its sector, and Italian courts and inspectors treat it that way.

A CCNL typically sets:

- job classification and levels;
- minimum pay for each level;
- normal working hours and overtime rules;
- paid leave, extra hours off and public holidays;
- probation length and notice periods;
- how sick pay is topped up;
- extra monthly payments such as the 13th and 14th month;
- training, union rights and sector funds for health and pension.

## The difference that matters: there is no opt-out

In Sweden, an employer that has not signed an agreement, directly or through an employer organisation, is in principle not bound by one. There is no legislation extending agreements to everybody. Many small foreign-owned companies operate that way for a while, and set pay and terms themselves.

In Italy you should not plan on that. The working assumption for any employer, small or large, foreign or local, is that the role is placed under the CCNL that fits the sector and the job, and that pay and conditions respect it. An Italian employee who is paid below the CCNL floor can claim the difference, and the claim grows month by month. Inspectors and courts look at the CCNL first.

So the question is never "do we have an agreement?" It is "which CCNL, and which level?"

## Choosing the agreement and the level

The CCNL follows the sector of the business and the nature of the work. It is not a menu where you take the cheapest option. A software company and a retail chain will not land on the same agreement, even when both hire an accountant.

Inside the agreement, the level drives the minimum pay and many linked terms. A junior analyst and a senior manager can sit under the same CCNL at very different levels. Promotions often mean a change of level, and with it a new minimum.

Some sectors have more than one agreement signed by different organisations. Picking between them needs care, because the choice affects cost for years.

## A floor, not a ceiling

As in Sweden, the agreement sets minimums. You can always offer more, and in competitive roles you will. You cannot offer less on any point the CCNL covers.

That has direct effects:

- **Salary.** The offer must be at or above the minimum for the level. Most skilled hires are paid above it, as "superminimo".
- **Cost.** The CCNL adds items Swedish finance teams do not expect: a 13th month in every agreement, a 14th in some, extra paid hours off, and contributions to sector funds.
- **Exit.** Notice comes from the CCNL, not from statute as it does under LAS.
- **Disputes.** The CCNL is the first document a judge reads.

## What carries over from your Swedish experience

Quite a lot. You already accept that a third-party document shapes the employment relationship. You already expect a pension and insurance layer on top of salary. You already know that local unions may have a role. Italian HR people will find you easier to work with than a manager from a system without bargaining.

What does not carry over is the assumption that terms can be negotiated freely with the employee where no agreement has been signed. In Italy, the individual contract sits on top of the CCNL, never beside it.

## Practical steps

1. **Map the role before the salary.** Sector, CCNL and level first; then the number.
2. **Read the agreement, not a summary from another country.** Swedish practice is a good instinct, not a guide to the Italian text.
3. **Budget the full cost.** Include 13th month, any 14th, extra leave, sector funds and employer contributions.
4. **Name the CCNL and the level in the offer and the contract.**
5. **Recheck on promotion.** A new level can mean new minimums.
