# Fixed-term or permanent in Italy? A decision guide for Nordic employers

> Italy, like Sweden, treats the open-ended contract as the norm. When a fixed-term contract fits, when it backfires, and what it means for retention.

Swedish employers know that the open-ended contract is the default and that fixed-term forms are limited. Special fixed-term and substitute employment are each capped at 12 months within a five-year period before they convert to indefinite employment ([Business Sweden](https://www.business-sweden.com/497a82/contentassets/582cb98533254f61bce11da9af436cf8/2026/employing-staff---contracts-and-conditions-2026.pdf)).

Italy follows the same philosophy. The permanent contract is the standard form, and fixed-term work is the exception with rules. The instinct you have from home is right. The specific limits are different, and a mistake has the same consequence: conversion into a permanent contract.

## What a fixed-term contract gives you

An end date. When the term ends, the relationship ends, without the reasons and procedure needed to dismiss a permanent employee.

That is the real benefit, and it is narrower than many founders hope:

- there is a cap on total duration;
- renewals and extensions are limited;
- beyond an initial period, a documented reason is generally required;
- breaking the rules can convert the contract into a permanent one;
- fixed-term employees have the same core rights as permanent staff.

The limits change from time to time. We check the current rules for each case.

## What a permanent contract gives you

Retention and a cleaner offer. Italian candidates value stability highly; a permanent contract from a foreign company with no Italian office already needs some trust. Offering a fixed term on top can make a strong candidate choose a local employer.

And the probation period of up to about six months, set by the CCNL, gives you a real window to assess fit. Ended correctly, it is the simplest exit there is.

## When fixed-term fits

- **Replacing someone on leave.** Parental leave is the classic case, and Nordic companies will use it often.
- **A defined project** with a clear end, such as a product launch or a trial programme.
- **Seasonal peaks**, for example around trade fairs or fashion weeks.
- **Market tests**, with care: if the test succeeds, plan the conversion to permanent in time.

## When it backfires

- Using a fixed term "to be safe" for a role you expect to keep. You lose candidates and gain little.
- Renewing again and again. Conversion risk grows with every renewal.
- Treating fixed-term staff as a cheaper tier. Pay, leave and protections are the same.

## Cost

A fixed-term contract does not save money. Pay, contributions, 13th month and TFR are the same. Fixed-term contracts can also carry an additional employer contribution . We show it in the quote.

## Part-time as an alternative

If the uncertainty is about workload rather than duration, part-time may be better. Italian part-time contracts are simple, and hours can be increased later with a written amendment.

## Decision in three questions

1. Do you expect the role to exist in two years? Then permanent, with a well-used probation period.
2. Is there a specific, documentable reason for an end date? Then fixed-term may fit.
3. Is the uncertainty about hours? Then consider part-time.

## Questions we hear from Nordic companies

**Can we convert a fixed-term contract to permanent early?**
Yes, and it is often the best outcome. A written amendment converts it, and the time already worked counts for seniority.

**Does a fixed-term employee get a probation period?**
A probation period can be agreed in a fixed-term contract too, proportionate to the length of the contract and within the CCNL limits.

**What if a parental-leave replacement turns out to be excellent?**
Plan ahead. When the employee on leave returns, the replacement contract ends on its terms. If you want to keep the replacement, offer a new permanent role before the end date.

## In short

Use permanent contracts for roles you expect to keep, and rely on a well-run probation period to manage the risk. Use fixed-term contracts where there is a real reason for an end date, such as cover for parental leave or a defined project, and track renewals closely. In Italy, as in Sweden, a fixed-term contract used out of caution tends to cost you good candidates without giving much real flexibility in return.
